# Review of Rafael

The manuscript presents a vision-based closed-loop positioning system for stepper-actuated laser guides, in which a camera observes the projected lines and corrects their position until they match targets defined in a CAD job file. The topic is relevant, however, the reported errors are not put into context with respect to the manual methods the system is meant to replace, the vision processing implementation is not sufficiently evidenced, and the manuscript is dense and difficult to follow.

- The introduction motivates the work by stating that manual measurement and marking are still commonly used. However, the errors reported for the proposed system (about 2 to 4 mm) are never compared with those of manual measurement and marking, so the reader cannot judge whether they are large or small, or whether the system improves on the method it is meant to replace. Please provide this comparison, supported by references or by an experiment. If the main benefit lies elsewhere, such as speed or reduced dependence on operator skill, please state and demonstrate it. Also, indicate the tolerances required by the target applications (welding, cutting, drilling, assembly) and whether the system meets them.
- There is no evidence of the vision processing implementation, which is part of the claimed contributions, beyond a brief mention that the lines are segmented by color (Section III-C). Please provide more details, such as how the calibration plate corners are detected and matched, how the laser lines are segmented (color space and thresholds), the software used, example images of the intermediate processing results, and the processing time per image, which according to Section IV-A was not recorded.
- The manuscript is very dense, particularly Sections IV-A to IV-D, where a large number of numerical values are reported in running text, which makes the main findings hard to extract. Please consider moving secondary values into tables and adding a short, intuitive explanation of the analysis and of what the results mean in practice.
- Minor: the fourth paragraph of the introduction begins with "These results suggest". Please consider stating explicitly which of the previously cited findings are meant.
- Minor: the abstract states "In 240 positioning runs on a prototype with two motorized line lasers [...], every closed-loop run met its tolerance within two moves". As only 120 of the 240 runs were performed in closed loop, with the other 120 in open loop, please consider rephrasing to make this split explicit (e.g., "In 240 positioning runs, 120 in open loop and 120 in closed loop, [...]").

# Review of Claude

The manuscript presents a vision-based closed-loop positioning system for low-cost, stepper-actuated laser guides. A fixed camera registers the workspace plane through a calibration plate of known geometry, task features from a DXF job file are mapped into the image as targets, and a quantized look-and-move law drives each laser until its projected line matches its target. The authors derive a convergence condition under sensitivity mismatch and a lower bound on the achievable tolerance, and report 240 runs on a two-laser prototype comparing open-loop and closed-loop positioning. The manuscript is well written, technically careful and easy to follow, and the authors are commendably transparent about its limitations. However, the experimental validation relies on the same camera that closes the loop, the evaluated scenario (two vertical lines on a flat panel, no workpiece) is considerably narrower than the manufacturing context suggested by the title and introduction, and the positioning of the work against existing laser projection and projector-camera literature could be strengthened.

1. The title refers to "CAD-Referenced Manufacturing", and the abstract and introduction mention welding paths, cutting lines, circular cutouts, drilling positions and assembly boundaries. However, the experiments use no workpiece (so the workpiece registration in Eq. (3) is never exercised), only vertical lines are projected, and no manufacturing task is performed. Please consider revising the title so that it reflects the scope actually validated, or extending the experiments to include at least one workpiece and one non-line pattern (e.g., a circle).
2. Please avoid acronyms in the title and abstract. "CAD" appears in the title, and the abstract uses "DXF" and "STEP/DIR" without spelling them out.
3. All errors, including the "final" closed-loop errors, are measured by the same camera and registration that close the loop. Since the loop terminates when the camera-measured error is below ε, the reported final errors are bounded by construction (as the authors acknowledge in Section IV-B). Please consider validating the final line positions with an independent measurement (e.g., a calibrated ruler or scale on the wall, a second camera, or a coordinate measuring device), so that the accuracy claims do not depend solely on the feedback sensor.
4. The wall-frame accuracy (Table II, last row, and Section IV-D) is an analytical estimate based on Eq. (12), not a measurement. Given that the estimated closed-loop wall RMSE (3.88 mm) is close to the 4 mm tolerance of laser 1, please clarify how many closed-loop runs would meet their tolerance on the wall, and consider registering directly on the wall plane (or compensating for the known plate thickness) in the experiments rather than leaving it to future work, since the correction appears straightforward.
5. The homography is estimated from exactly four plate corners, so its residual is zero and gives no information on registration accuracy; moreover, the targets lie outside the plate and near the image edges, where uncorrected lens distortion is largest. Please quantify the registration error at the target locations (e.g., with a checkerboard or additional fiducials) and justify not correcting lens distortion, given that standard calibration [11] is inexpensive.
6. In 100 of the 120 closed-loop runs the first move already met the tolerance, so the final error in most runs is identical to the open-loop error and is mainly determined by the choice of ε. Please discuss how the reported reductions (e.g., 51% in RMSE) depend on ε, and consider reporting results for a common, tighter tolerance closer to the derived floor (about 2.9 to 3.0 mm).
7. The two lasers use different tolerances (4 and 6 mm), which the authors themselves identify as the cause of the larger final error of laser 2. This makes the per-laser comparison difficult to interpret. Please justify this choice or repeat the experiments with the same tolerance for both lasers.
8. The tolerances are said to be "chosen from the backlash (lost motion on reversal) of each drive", but the lasers are mounted directly on the motor shafts without gearing. Please clarify the source of this lost motion and how it was measured.
9. The open-loop and first closed-loop moves are stated to be identical, yet their RMSE differs considerably (6.57 versus 4.53 mm), which the authors attribute to variation between trials. This suggests a large trial-dependent bias (per-trial intercepts from −5.3 to 4.1 mm). Please explain the origin of this variation (e.g., homing, mounting, thermal effects, missed steps), describe whether open-loop and closed-loop trials were interleaved or randomized, and consider adding a statistical test to support the comparison.
10. The open-loop baseline uses a constant sensitivity estimate that ignores the tangent geometry of the rotary projector. A fairer baseline would be an open-loop model that includes this geometry (and possibly a one-time calibration). Please consider adding such a baseline, or discuss why it was not included, since part of the reported improvement may come from the simplified open-loop model.
11. Please clarify whether all commanded moves were in the same direction. Section II-B gives "160 mm to the right" as an example, and Eq. (4) defines the sign relative to "the direction of the commanded moves". If only one direction was tested, direction-dependent effects such as lost motion on reversal were not evaluated in the first move.
12. The microstepping resolution is set to 4 microsteps per full step, which gives about 3.9 mm per microstep on the wall, i.e., about one microstep for the 4 mm tolerance. Since the TMC2209 supports up to 256 microsteps, please justify this coarse setting, as the derived tolerance floor is dominated by |Ĵ|/2 and would decrease substantially with finer microstepping.
13. Please detail the computation behind the bound of "about 3.1 and 3.2 mm" after one correction (Section IV-A). Applying Eq. (7) with c = 0.022 and 0.034, |J| ≈ 3.9 mm and the stated noise allowances seems to yield somewhat smaller values; if a different |J| (e.g., at oblique incidence) was used, please state it explicitly. A similar clarification would help for the tolerance floors of about 2.9 and 3.0 mm.
14. Condition (9) is derived for 0 < λρ ≤ 1, whereas the experiments report λρ ≈ 1.02 to 1.05. Please state explicitly the expression used in this regime, so the reader can verify the experimental values against the theory.
15. The settling time of 1.5 s dominates each iteration, while the longest move takes less than 0.1 s, and image processing times were not recorded. Please report the processing time and justify the settling time, since cycle time is relevant for practical use at manual workstations.
16. The second paragraph of Section IV-E lists several effects that "were not measured" (robustness to displaced workpieces, disturbed mounts, missed steps, ambient light, reflective surfaces). Since robustness to actuator and projector-model errors is the main motivation for closing the loop, please consider adding at least one experiment with a deliberate disturbance (e.g., a displaced laser mount or injected missed steps) to support this claim.
17. The state of the art would benefit from a broader review of (i) projector-camera and laser projector calibration and registration for spatial augmented reality and industrial guidance, (ii) closed-loop or feedback-based laser projection systems, and (iii) visual servoing with quantized or stepper-motor actuation. This would help justify the novelty of treating the projected pattern as the controlled variable.
18. Please position the achieved accuracy (about 2 to 4 mm) with respect to the requirements of the target applications (welding, drilling, cutting, assembly) and to the accuracy of commercial laser projectors or galvanometer-based systems [3], [4], [16], [17], ideally with a quantitative comparison table.
19. The opening statement that marking with rulers, templates and scribes "is still commonly done" and is "time-consuming and makes the accuracy dependent on operator skill" is not supported by a citation. Please add a reference.
20. Several core references are old ([6] 1996, [11] 2000, [12] 2004, [18] 1991, [21] 2000). They are foundational and appropriate, but please consider complementing them with recent works, particularly on quantized feedback and visual servoing, to show that the problem remains current.
21. Please spell out all acronyms on first use in the main text, for example UART, STEP/DIR, ROI (Table I), USB, NEMA and 8N1, and introduce DXF before its first use in Table I.
22. The scalar quantities ρ and c are defined only for the single-feature case, while the control law (5) is written with matrices Ĵᵢ and their pseudo-inverse. Please clarify whether the multi-feature case (e.g., line angle or circle center) is supported by the analysis, or restrict the claims accordingly.
23. The font sizes in Figs. 1, 4 and 5 (axis labels, legends and annotations such as ε and ν̄) are small and hard to read at print size. Please enlarge them. In Fig. 3, please consider not relying on color alone to distinguish the blocks running on each processor.
24. The conclusions state that "actuator and projector-model errors are corrected by feedback". Since missed steps and model disturbances were not experimentally introduced, please qualify this statement as an analytical expectation, consistent with Section IV-E.
25. Please consider making the experimental data, DXF job files and code publicly available to support reproducibility, given that no public dataset or external benchmark is used.
