# Writing Strong Research Objectives | Criteria & Examples

A **research objective** describes what your research is trying to achieve and explains why you are pursuing it. Objectives summarize the approach and purpose of your project and help to focus your research.

Your objectives should appear in the introduction of your research paper, at the end of your problem statement. They should:

- **Establish** the scope and depth of your project
- **Contribute** to your research design
- **Indicate** how your project will contribute to existing knowledge

All research objectives should be:

- **Specific** — clearly defined, not overly vague
- **Measurable** — you know how to assess whether they have been achieved
- **Achievable** — feasible given your timeframe and available resources
- **Relevant** — directly address the research problem and contribute to the field
- **Time-based** — bounded by clear deadlines to keep the project on track

## Research Aim vs. Research Objectives

A **research aim** is a broad statement indicating the general purpose of your project. Your **research objectives** are more specific and indicate the particular focus and approach. Though you will have only one research aim, you will likely have several objectives.

| | Research Aim | Research Objectives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Scope | Broad, general purpose | Specific, actionable steps |
| Quantity | One | Several |
| Role | Sets direction | Breaks down the path to get there |

### Example

| Example research aim | Example research objectives |
| --- | --- |
| To examine contributory factors to muscle retention in a group of elderly people. | - To assess the relationship between sedentary habits and muscle atrophy among the participants. |
| | - To determine the impact of dietary factors, particularly protein consumption, on the muscular health of the participants. |
| | - To determine the effect of physical activity on the participants' muscular health. |

## Why Are Research Objectives Important?

| Reason | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Establish scope and depth | Helps you avoid unnecessary research and makes your methods and conclusions easy to evaluate. |
| Contribute to research design | Knowing your objectives clarifies which methods are most appropriate. |
| Indicate contribution to extant research | Allows you to display knowledge of current literature, employ recent methods, and engage with ongoing debates. |

## How to Write Research Aims and Objectives

### Step 1 — Decide on a general aim
Your research aim should reflect your research problem and remain relatively broad.

> **Example aim:** To assess the safety features and response times of self-driving cars.

### Step 2 — Decide on specific objectives
Break down your aim into a limited number of steps that will help you resolve the research problem. Ask: what specific aspects of the problem do you want to examine or understand?

> **Example objective:** To measure the response time of self-driving cars to pedestrians.

### Step 3 — Formulate your aims and objectives
State them clearly and concisely at the end of your problem statement. Frame them as declarative statements and use precise action verbs.

> **Example formulation:** The aim of this research is to assess the safety features and response times of self-driving cars. Using quantitative methods, I will measure the response time of self-driving cars, first to other moving vehicles and then to pedestrians. I will also test their awareness of nearby inanimate obstacles (e.g., sidewalks).

## What Makes a Strong Research Objective?

### Clarity and precision

| Criteria | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Uses measurable action words | Replace vague terms like _understand_ or _explore_ with precise verbs: _determine_, _assess_, _measure_, _compare_, _evaluate_. |
| Avoids value judgements | Avoid subjective words like _good_, _bad_, _better_, _worse_. These do not give clear criteria for evaluation. |
| One question per objective | Each objective should address one specific aspect. Test whether it could stand as its own manuscript section. |

### Independence

| Criteria | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Each objective stands alone | Avoid creating dependencies where completing one objective requires another to be finished first. A cascading failure in one should not derail the entire project. |
| No overlap | Objectives should be complementary but not redundant — each should add a distinct dimension to the research aim. |

### Achievability and relevance

| Criteria | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Feasible within constraints | Ensure access to the data, tools, and time needed to meet each objective. Narrow the scope if access is uncertain. |
| Grounded in existing literature | Each objective should connect to a gap or debate in the field. Review how other researchers in your area structure their objectives. |
| Contributes beyond the project | Strong objectives produce knowledge that future researchers or practitioners can build on. |

## SMART Checklist

Use this checklist to evaluate each objective before finalising:

| SMART criterion | Question to ask |
| --- | --- |
| **S**pecific | Is the objective clearly defined and unambiguous? |
| **M**easurable | How will I know when this objective has been achieved? |
| **A**chievable | Do I have the resources, data, and time to complete this? |
| **R**elevant | Does this directly address my research problem and contribute to the field? |
| **T**ime-based | Is there a clear deadline or phase in which this will be completed? |

## Using Sub-Objectives

If a primary objective is too broad to address in a single step, break it into sub-objectives. Good sub-objectives should be:

- Less complex than the parent objective
- Focused on one aspect or method
- Presented in a logical order

Limit yourself to 3–4 sub-objectives per primary objective. If you need more, revisit the parent objective and tighten its scope.

## Common Verbs by Objective Type

| Purpose | Appropriate verbs |
| --- | --- |
| Describing and exploring | characterise, describe, identify, map, document |
| Explaining and testing | determine, examine, analyse, measure, test, assess |
| Evaluating and acting | evaluate, compare, appraise, validate, optimise |
