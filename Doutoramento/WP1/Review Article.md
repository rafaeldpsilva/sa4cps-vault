# AI-Driven Occupant Modeling in Intelligent Built Environments: A Systematic Review

## Abstract
As intelligent built environments move toward anticipating the needs of their occupants, the occupant becomes the central object of modeling, yet most systems capture only a thin proxy of them: a bundle of comfort preferences and setpoints rather than a coherent account of who the occupant is. This review asks which AI methods have been applied to dynamically model, evolve, and reason over occupant preferences and latent intent, at individual and collective levels within physical instrumented spaces, and how far the two method families with the most apparent potential for this deeper modeling, relational approaches (knowledge graphs, ontologies, graph neural networks) and generative approaches (large language and foundation models), have been explored. Following the PRISMA guidelines, three databases were searched over a recent publication window and screened against explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria. For each included study, the following were extracted: the occupant dimension modeled, the AI method family, whether the model is static or evolving, the evaluation approach, and any handling of multiple occupants. The landscape is dominated by established techniques, classical machine learning and multi-component hybrid systems, while relational and generative methods, though present and rising, remain secondary and rarely serve as the representational backbone. Dynamic modeling leans heavily on explicit user feedback, the least scalable trigger, and work addressing multiple occupants or validated in real deployments is scarce. The empty regions of this landscape expose three structural gaps: the absence of relational user, environment, and context representations driven by live data; the confinement of generative AI to interface roles rather than reasoning over the occupant model; and the near-total absence of integrated, evolving persona modeling. These gaps are not independent but compound into one missing capability, pointing toward a relational, generative synthesis: a continuously updated, graph-structured occupant persona over which a generative model reasons to anticipate unstated needs.
## 1. Introduction

Built environments are shifting from reactive control to proactive, occupant-aware intelligence. For decades, a "smart" building meant one that responded to measurable conditions: adjusting a thermostat to a set temperature, or switching on lights when it detected occupancy. The ambition now is anticipatory: environments that understand the people inside them and act on that understanding before being asked. This shift relocates the object of modeling from the environment to the occupant.

Yet most systems that claim to model the occupant in fact model only a thin proxy of them. They capture what a resident prefers (a comfortable temperature, a lighting level, a schedule) but not who the resident is: their personality, how they like to interact with systems, what they expect, and how all of this changes over time and across contexts. The occupant is flattened into a set of setpoints. The distinction this review draws throughout is between modeling a *resident* (a bundle of preferences) and modeling a *persona* (a coherent, evolving person), and it is the gap between the two that defines the field's frontier.

This motivates the review's question: what AI methods have been applied to dynamically model, evolve, and reason over occupant preferences and latent intent, at individual and collective levels, within intelligent built environments, and to what extent have relational and generative AI approaches been explored? The question is deliberately two-part: it maps the methods in use, and it asks specifically how far the two method families with the most apparent potential for persona-level modeling, relational approaches (knowledge graphs, ontologies, GNNs) and generative approaches (LLMs, foundation models), have actually been taken up.

The scope is AI-driven occupant modeling situated in physical, instrumented spaces: smart homes, buildings, offices, and communities. Included is any work that uses an AI method to model or infer an occupant's preferences, behavioral patterns, contextual needs, or latent intent beyond physical setpoints, at either the individual or the collective level. Excluded is purely environmental modeling (occupancy sensing, thermal comfort, energy optimization) with no occupant-preference layer, and user profiling for web-platform purposes (social media, e-commerce, news) with no built-environment grounding. The inclusion criteria (IC) and exclusion criteria (EC) are given in Section 2.

This review makes three contributions. It maps the AI-method landscape of occupant modeling against a three-level taxonomy of modeling depth, through a cross-tabulation (Section 4.1) that exposes where the field is dense and where it is empty. It identifies three structural gaps from those empty regions, the absence of relational user–environment–context representations, the narrow use of generative AI for reasoning over preferences, and the near-total absence of integrated persona modeling. It argues that these gaps are not independent but compound into a single missing capability, pointing to a relational–generative convergence as the research direction the landscape itself motivates.
## 2. Methodology
This review follows the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for systematic reviews [@page2021prisma]. The protocol comprises a structured database search, a screening procedure against explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria, and a predefined data-extraction schema. Each stage is documented below for transparency.
### 2.1 Search Strategy
The search targeted the intersection of two literatures that rarely co-index: user modeling from computer science, and the built-environment and ambient-intelligence literature. Three databases were queried, Web of Science (WoS), the ACM Digital Library, and IEEE Xplore, chosen for their complementary coverage of human–computer interaction, applied AI, and built-environment engineering.
A common conceptual query was adapted to each database's syntax, pairing user-modeling terms with a built-environment context filter. Bellow are the used queries for adapted to each database syntax:
- WoS: `(ALL=("user modeling" OR "user profiling") AND ALL=(intelligent OR smart OR cognitive) AND ALL=(home* OR communit* OR building*))`
- ACM: `("user modeling" OR "user profiling") AND profile AND preferences AND (intelligent OR smart OR cognitive) AND (home* OR communit* OR building*)`, E-Publication Date within the last five years.
- **IEEE Xplore:** `("user modeling" OR "user profiling") AND profile AND preferences AND (intelligent OR smart OR cognitive) AND (home* OR communit* OR building*))`
The searches were executed on April 2026. Two filters were applied at source: publication within the last five years, and, where possible, document type restricted to research papers. This five-year window is the binding date constraint and is stricter than the explicit cut-off in EC5 (Section 2.2). The searches returned 55 records from WoS, 404 from ACM, and 174 from IEEE Xplore, for 633 records in total.
### 2.2 Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
A record was included only if it satisfied all of the following:
- IC1, Publication type: peer-reviewed primary research study, a journal article or full conference paper reporting an original occupant model or system (not a literature review or survey, nor a workshop summary, poster, or extended abstract).
- IC2, Language: written in English.
- IC3, AI method: applies at least one AI method (machine learning, knowledge representation, probabilistic model, or generative architecture) to model or infer occupant-level information, not solely to optimize physical parameters.
- IC4, Human dimension: models or infers at least one dimension of occupant preference, behavioral pattern, contextual need, or latent intent beyond physical setpoints, comfort preferences, interaction preferences, temporal behavioral patterns, intent signals, or collective/group dynamics.
- IC5, Context: situated in a built environment (smart building, smart home, intelligent environment, smart community, or equivalent instrumented physical space).
A record was excluded if any of the following held:
- EC1, Environment-only: exclusively physical or environmental modeling (occupancy sensing, thermal comfort, energy, HVAC) with no occupant preference or intent layer.
- EC2, Web platform: user profiling for web-platform purposes (ad targeting, sentiment analysis, social-media or e-commerce recommendation) with no built-environment application.
- EC3, No learned representation: pure rule-based or sensor-threshold systems producing no learned occupant model, profile, or preference representation.
- EC4, Non-paper: workshop summaries, keynote abstracts, editorials, or records with no retrievable abstract.
- EC5, Out of date: published before 2019.
The AI-method requirement (IC3) is an inclusion gate only: it ensures a learned representation exists. The *type* of method (relational, generative, classical) is not a gate but a data-extraction variable, used in the synthesis (Section 4) to characterize how the landscape is distributed and where relational and generative approaches remain underexplored.

### 2.3 Screening Process

The 633 records were deduplicated, removing 8 duplicates and yielding 625 unique records.

Each unique record was screened against the inclusion and exclusion criteria on the basis of its title and abstract. The dominant reason for exclusion was web-platform recommendation work (EC2), social-media, e-commerce, and news-recommendation studies that share user-modeling methods but lack any built-environment application. A second large group fell to context mismatch: recommendation systems, metaverse and gaming, and cognitive-radio or hardware work that superficially matched the query terms but lay outside the built-environment scope. Title-and-abstract screening excluded 572 records, leaving 53 sought for full-text assessment.

Full-text assessment of the 53 candidates applied the criteria to the complete article. Eight records were excluded: four literature reviews or survey papers (failing IC1, as they report no original occupant model), and four whose domain fell outside the built-environment scope on close reading (retail-marketing push, smart-city attitude surveys, interface-design studies, and privacy-tension analyses without a learned occupant representation). This left **45 papers** in the final corpus.

### 2.4 Data Extraction

For each included paper, five variables were extracted into a structured table:

1. **Occupant dimension**, the human aspect modeled (comfort preference, behavioral pattern, interaction style, latent intent, collective/group dynamics).
2. **AI method**, the method family (classical ML, deep learning, probabilistic/statistical, relational [KG/ontology/GNN], generative [LLM/foundation], reinforcement learning, hybrid).
3. **Preference evolution**, whether the occupant model is static or evolves over time (dynamic).
4. **Evaluation type**, simulation, dataset benchmark, user study, or real deployment (with participant count where reported).
5. **Multi-occupant handling**, whether the work addresses shared-space conflict or negotiation, and the mechanism used.

These variables feed the cross-tabulation of AI method against occupant-modeling depth that anchors the synthesis (Section 4.1).

### 2.6 Limitations of the Review Process

Three constraints qualify the corpus and should be read alongside the results. First, screening was performed by a single reviewer, without an independent second screener or a formal measure of inter-rater agreement; borderline inclusion and the assignment of data-extraction categories (Section 2.4) therefore reflect a single judgement. Second, the review was not pre-registered (for example in PROSPERO), so the protocol was not externally fixed in advance. Third, the search drew on three databases, WoS, ACM, and IEEE Xplore, chosen for coverage but not exhaustive; relevant work indexed only elsewhere (e.g. Scopus or Springer) may be absent. These constraints bound the completeness of the corpus and are revisited in the Discussion.
## 3. Results

### 3.1 Corpus Overview
The 45 included papers span 2021–2026: 6 in 2021, 3 in 2022, 6 in 2023, 10 in 2024, 16 in 2025, and 4 in 2026. The counts rise toward the recent end, but this must be read cautiously rather than as a clean growth curve: the five-year source filter truncates earlier work and the 2026 figure is a partial year at the search date, both of which mechanically front-load the recent years. With that caveat, the concentration of the corpus in 2024–2025 (26 of 45) indicates that occupant modeling beyond physical comfort is an active and intensifying concern rather than a settled field. The work is also venue-fragmented, drawn in roughly equal measure from human–computer interaction, applied-AI, and built-environment outlets, which explains why no single database returned the full picture and motivated the three-database search.

This recency has a methodological consequence visible throughout the results: the newest papers are precisely those introducing relational and generative methods, so the landscape is not static but tilting. The distributions reported below are therefore best read as a snapshot of a field in transition.

### 3.2 AI Method Landscape

Classifying each paper by primary method family yields a long-tailed distribution dominated by established techniques. Classical machine learning (clustering, classification, regression) accounts for 11 papers and remains the single most common pure method, applied to tasks from speech-emotion recognition [Ma 2022] to navigation-based personality inference [Araujo & Junior 2026]. A further 3 papers use standalone deep learning for behavioral sequence and survey modeling [Constantinescu & Iftene 2025; Jia 2025; Shukla 2025], and 2 use probabilistic or statistical models for latent-parameter inference [Freire 2021; Wu & Jokinen 2025]. Together these established families cover 16 of 45 papers.

The methods central to this review's analytical question are present but secondary. Relational approaches, such as knowledge graphs, ontologies, and GNNs, account for 8 papers [e.g. Asprino 2024; Festus 2024; Javdani Rikhtehgar 2023; Liu 2023a; Liu 2023b; Umbrico 2021; Liu 2025], and generative approaches built on LLMs or foundation models account for 6 [Danry 2026; Huang 2025; Javdani Rikhtehgar 2025; Tamah Al-Shammari 2025; Tsihrintzis 2025; Zhao & Silverajan 2024]. Reinforcement learning appears once as a standalone method [Md Zuki 2025] in persuasive systems.

The largest single category, however, is hybrid (14 papers), and this is the key reading of the landscape: relational and generative techniques most often enter the field not as standalone architectures but folded into multi-component systems, an LLM injecting world knowledge into a Bayesian network [Han 2025], an ontology paired with contextual bandits [Stefan 2021; Liu 2024], or symbolic trust reasoning over evolving personas [Vozna 2025]. Counting these embedded uses (5 of the 14 hybrids fold in a knowledge graph, ontology, or LLM component [Han 2025; Jin 2026; Liu 2024; Tsitseklis 2023; Vozna 2025]), relational and generative techniques touch 19 of the 45 papers (42%), though in roughly a third of those cases as supporting components rather than the representational backbone. Classical ML still dominates wherever the modeling target is well-defined; relational and generative methods cluster where the target is semantically rich or linguistically expressed. This split, made explicit in the cross-tabulation of Section 4.1, is the central empirical finding of the review.

### 3.3 Occupant Modeling Depth

Not every "occupant model" models at the same depth. We distinguish three approaches: Level 1, physical setpoints, comfort, and demographics (preferred illuminance, temperature schedules, demographic tags); Level 2, behavioral patterns and interaction styles (activity routines, navigation behavior, modality preferences); and Level 3, cognitive, personality, and latent-intent modeling (Big Five traits, trust, expectations, unarticulated needs). These tiers are an analytical construct rather than a label drawn from the papers themselves, and borderline cases exist, an accessibility scan that adapts to a user's mobility needs, for instance, sits between Levels 1 and 2.

The corpus concentrates at the deeper tiers: 24 papers operate at Level 3, 16 at Level 2, and only 5 at Level 1. This skew is in large part by construction, IC4 deliberately excludes papers that model nothing beyond physical setpoints, so Level 1 here contains only the few papers that attach a comfort layer to a richer occupant representation [e.g. Delle Monache 2022; Festus 2024; Stefan 2021]. The distribution therefore cannot be read as a field-wide tendency; selection makes Level 1 sparse by design. What it does show is that, within work that already models the occupant beyond setpoints, behavioral and cognitive constructs are well represented rather than marginal, the studied subfield has the appetite to model who the occupant is, even if, as Section 3.5 shows, the methods for doing so reliably remain immature.

### 3.4 Preference Dynamics

Static profiles are insufficient because occupants change across time and context; 28 of 45 papers model some form of evolution, against 17 that treat the occupant profile as fixed. Static approaches rely on cold-start personas [Tamah Al-Shammari 2025], demographic classification from historical traces [Liu 2023a; Tang 2024], or one-shot survey instruments [Shukla 2025].

The dynamic papers divide by adaptation trigger. The most common trigger is explicit feedback: the occupant directly tells the system to adjust, via end-of-task ratings [Umbrico 2021], textual corrections to environmental scans [Huang 2025], or calibration surveys [Delle Monache 2022]. A second group adapts to implicit behavioral drift, updating silently as interaction patterns shift, inferring interest from dwell time on a display [Freire 2021], logging reading and backspacing patterns to revise a psychological profile [Araujo & Junior 2026], or triggering updates when clicks diverge from prior assumptions [Virvou & Tsihrintzis 2025]. A third, smaller group adapts to context change, where situational framing reshapes preference: a shift from partial to full vehicle autonomy flipping skeptics' preferences [Cham & Kefalidou 2025], a pressure-pad time window activating a sleep-lighting profile [Festus 2024], or transient emotional state altering music choice [Tran 2021].

The distribution matters: dynamic modeling exists but leans heavily on explicit feedback, the most burdensome and least scalable trigger. Implicit and context-driven adaptation, the mechanisms most relevant to unobtrusive built environments, are comparatively rare.

The static/dynamic coding reflects each paper's claimed modeling behavior and is author-judged; a few papers coded as dynamic argue for adaptivity at the conceptual level without demonstrating empirical evolution of the profile, for example, Md Zuki et al. [2025] propose a sequential-decision framework but report no empirical evaluation.

### 3.5 Latent Intent Inference

The hardest modeling problem in the corpus is inferring what an occupant wants but has not stated. The papers that attempt it are methodologically diverse but mutually isolated, each importing a technique from a different tradition. Wu & Jokinen [2025] treat the occupant as a bounded-rational agent and recover hidden preference and expertise through Approximate Bayesian Inverse Reinforcement Learning, matching summary statistics of observed behavior against simulated trajectories. Han et al. [2025] model intent as a hidden variable in a Dynamic Bayesian Network updated from noisy gaze and touch streams. Freire et al. [2021] invert Information Foraging Theory, fitting attention-maximization parameters to viewing times to estimate interest without any interaction. Danry et al. [2026] use a multi-stage LLM pipeline as an inductive reasoner, mining human-readable "if–then" behavioral rules from conversational audio. Liu et al. [2023a] learn low-dimensional behavioral embeddings via graph and tensor methods.

The significance is in the absence of convergence: five papers, five unrelated formalisms, no shared evaluation, no common representation of "intent." Latent-intent inference is recognized as central but lacks any unifying framework, a gap that the relational/generative convergence argued in Section 5 is positioned to fill.

### 3.6 Multi-Occupant Dynamics

Real built environments hold multiple occupants with competing preferences, yet only 5 of 45 papers engage multi-occupant settings at all; the remaining 40 model the occupant as an isolated entity. The five split into two distinct framings. Four address shared-space conflict or some form of negotiation directly [Tran 2021; Umbrico 2021; Langerak 2026; Danry 2026], while one models collective dynamics at community scale without an explicit conflict-resolution mechanism [Asprino 2024]. Even across these five, no paper combines social structure with algorithmic resolution.

Tran et al. [2021] give the most complete algorithmic treatment via group recommender systems, resolving conflicting preferences through least-misery optimization for high-stakes choices, average-voting for low-stakes ones, dynamic priority weights that elevate previously-overruled members for fairness, and explicit negotiation that weighs each user's assertiveness against cooperativeness. Asprino et al. [2024] model collective dynamics at community scale through an ontology of citizen curation, optimizing for community cohesion rather than individual satisfaction. The remaining three treat multiplicity as context rather than as something to resolve algorithmically: Langerak et al. [2026] model a partner's hidden state (fatigue, mood) and prioritize explaining it to prevent misaligned expectations; Danry et al. [2026] predict how an occupant will respond to interpersonal friction without resolving the conflict; and Umbrico et al. [2021] schedule human–robot task allocation to minimize cycle time in a shared workspace.

The pattern is that multi-occupant work is fragmented across two disjoint framings, algorithmic preference aggregation on one side, social and contextual modeling on the other, with no paper integrating power dynamics, social structure, and computational resolution in a single framework.

The grid reads two ways. Along the depth axis, the corpus thins sharply at Level 1 (5 papers) and thickens toward Level 3 (24), the selection-driven skew already noted in Section 3.3. Along the method axis, the density sits in two regions: classical ML applied to cognitive and behavioral targets (10 of its 11 papers), and hybrid systems spread across all three depths (14 papers). Relational and generative methods are present at every depth but never dominate a column.

Six cells are empty, and the pattern in the gaps is as informative as the clusters. **Deep learning is absent at both Level 1 and Level 3**: it is used for observable behavioral sequences (navigation, survey representation) but not for physical setpoints, where simpler models suffice, nor for cognitive traits, where the black-box interpretability problem (Section 3.2) makes pure neural networks unsuitable for trust-sensitive inference, there, the field reaches for hybrids that pair learning with symbolic or generative components. **Probabilistic methods appear only at Level 3**: their computational overhead is justified only for the hardest latent-inference problems and would be wasteful for setpoints or direct behavioral tracking. **Reinforcement learning appears only once**, reflecting the difficulty of letting an agent explore and fail in a live, user-facing environment without a hybrid safety net. With a corpus of 45, an individual empty cell is suggestive rather than conclusive, since a single absence can reflect sampling as much as structure; the reading here therefore rests on the count agreeing with the methodological reasons each method is ill-suited to the missing cell, not on the count alone. Three structural gaps emerge from these empty and sparse regions, developed below.

### 4.2 Relational Representations of User–Environment–Context

The dominant paradigm represents the occupant as a flat feature vector or matrix: behaviors, environmental readings, and demographics arranged as independent dimensions. This discards the relational structure that actually carries meaning in a built environment, the dependencies between a user, the device they are addressing, the room they are in, the time of day, and the other occupants present. These are non-Euclidean relationships that vector models cannot represent and that knowledge graphs and GNNs are built to capture.

The corpus shows this capability emerging but not consolidating: 8 papers use relational methods directly and another 5 embed them within hybrids (Section 3.2), yet they remain a minority and cluster at the behavioral tier (Table 2). The recurring technical obstacle is integration: relational systems map semantic structure well [Festus 2024; Umbrico 2021; Asprino 2024] but struggle to fuse real-time numerical sensor streams with the symbolic graph, while the GNN work that handles numerical data well [Liu 2023a; Liu 2025] does so over static snapshots rather than live environmental context. Where relational systems do update from the environment, they do so on discrete event triggers rather than continuous streams; Festus et al. [2024], for example, revise an ontology when an IoT sensor fires. No paper maintains a relational occupant representation driven continuously by live sensor data, which is the representation a built environment ultimately requires.

### 4.3 Generative AI for Preference Elicitation and Reasoning

Generative models are the newest entrants (Section 3.1) and the corpus shows their use is still narrow. Of the 6 papers using LLMs directly, most confine them to conversational, evaluative, or content-generation roles, such as dashboard usability evaluation [Zhao 2024] and narrative generation [Tamah Al-Shammari 2025]. In these the LLM serves as an interface or content generator, not as a reasoning engine over the occupant model.

The unexplored capability is generative AI as an active reasoner for unarticulated needs. Two papers gesture at it: Danry et al. [2026] use an LLM as an inductive reasoner to mine human-readable "if–then" behavioral heuristics from conversation, and Han et al. [2025] inject LLM world knowledge into a Bayesian network when context shifts unexpectedly. These are isolated demonstrations, and both mine patterns passively from data already collected rather than actively eliciting what a user has not expressed. The corpus contains no system that uses a generative model to draw out unstated preferences on demand, to reason over a structured occupant representation, or to supply common sense world knowledge to the environment's decision-making as a standing component. Given that latent-intent inference is the field's hardest problem and currently lacks any unifying method (Section 3.5), the reasoning capacity of generative models is a conspicuously underused fit.

### 4.4 Modeling the Occupant as a "Persona"
The deepest gap is conceptual. Even at Level 3, most papers model fragments of the occupant, a single psychological trait, an expertise level, a trust score, rather than an integrated persona. Systems predict physiologically or behaviorally optimal responses but rarely represent who the occupant is: their personality, interaction style, cognitive expectations, and how these cohere and evolve. Yet the same literature establishes that these psychographic factors strongly shape technology acceptance and behavior [Cham & Kefalidou 2025; Ma 2022; Sankaran & Markopoulos 2021].

The rare exception proves the gap: Vozna et al. [2025] model evolving "Blueprint Personas" that adapt interaction style and prompt frequency as a patient's trust and condition change, an integrated, dynamic persona rather than a trait list. Persona-level modeling, integrating psychological traits, interaction preferences, and cognitive state into a single representation that evolves over time, remains a largely unoccupied frontier, and it is the move from modeling what an occupant wants to modeling who they are that motivates the research direction in Section 5.
## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 The Three Gaps Compound

The gaps identified in Section 4 are usually treated as separate research opportunities, but the central argument of this review is that they are not independent, they are layers of a single missing capability, and each blocks the next. Without a relational representation, there is no structure on which to assemble an integrated persona: a flat feature vector cannot express how a user's personality, their interaction history, the devices in a room, and the other occupants relate. Without an integrated persona, there is nothing rich enough for a generative model to reason over: an LLM asked to anticipate an unarticulated need has only disconnected traits to work from, not a coherent model of the person. On this reading, the empty and sparse cells of Table 2 are not three coincidental absences but the visible trace of one architectural omission: the field has no representation that is simultaneously relational, persona-level, and amenable to generative reasoning.
This reframes the scarcity of real deployments. It is not merely a sign of immaturity but a direct consequence of the same omission: the claims that most need validation in situ, that a relational persona improves anticipation, or that generative reasoning elicits genuine latent needs, are precisely the ones the current method stack cannot yet produce. What cannot be built cannot be deployed.

### 5.2 Toward Relational–Generative Convergence

The compounding structure also points to its resolution. If the gaps are layers of one capability, the research direction is their convergence rather than their separate pursuit: a graph-structured occupant persona that a generative model reasons over. Concretely, this means representing the occupant, their context, devices, and co-occupants as a heterogeneous graph that updates from live sensor streams, encoding psychological, interactional, and cognitive attributes as first-class nodes that evolve over time, and using a generative model not as a chat interface but as a reasoning engine that traverses and extends this graph to infer unstated needs and inject world knowledge when context shifts.

The corpus contains the ingredients in isolation: GNNs over user–environment graphs [Liu 2023a; Liu 2025], evolving persona representations [Vozna 2025], and LLMs as inductive reasoners [Danry 2026; Han 2025]. No work combines them, which marks this convergence as a clear direction for future research rather than an arbitrary architecture: it is the direction the empty regions of the landscape most directly suggest.

### 5.3 Limitations

Several limitations qualify these conclusions, beyond the review-process constraints already stated in Section 2.6. The corpus is small (45 papers) and the boundaries that produced it required judgement calls: deciding that museums, vehicles, and digital-health settings count as "built environments" for IC5, and that the occupant-depth tiers (Section 3.3) and method families (Section 3.2) map cleanly onto each paper. Different boundary decisions would shift individual placements in Table 2; the dominant pattern (established methods at the centre, relational and generative methods sparse and recent) appears stable across reasonable variations, though no formal sensitivity analysis was performed. Finally, the English-only and last-five-years filters bias the corpus toward recent, anglophone work, which likely understates older relational and knowledge-representation research that predates the window.

## 6. Conclusion

This review mapped how AI is used to model occupants in intelligent built environments across, classifying each by method family and modeling depth. The landscape is dominated by established methods, classical machine learning applied to behavioral and cognitive targets, and hybrid systems spread across all depths, while the two families with the clearest potential for persona-level modeling, relational and generative AI, remain a recent and sparse minority, touching fewer than half the corpus and rarely serving as the representational backbone.

From the empty regions of that landscape, three structural gaps emerge: the absence of relational representations that capture user–environment–context dependencies and update from live sensor streams; the confinement of generative AI to interface roles rather than reasoning over occupant models; and the near-total absence of integrated, evolving persona modeling. The review's central claim is that these gaps compound, relational structure is the precondition for a persona, and a persona is the precondition for generative reasoning over it, so that they describe not three separate deficits but one missing capability.

The direction this implies is convergence: a graph-structured, continuously updated occupant persona over which a generative model reasons to anticipate unstated needs. The ingredients exist in isolation across the corpus; none combines them, and that combination stands out as the most promising direction for future work. The broader point is a reorientation the field has begun but not completed: for an environment to be genuinely intelligent about the people within it, it must model not only what its occupants want, but who they are.
